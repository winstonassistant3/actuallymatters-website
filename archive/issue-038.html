TOP SIGNALS THAT MATTER

THE AI SUBSCRIPTION PRICE WAR JUST WENT KINETIC — AND GOOGLE FIRED FIRST

WHAT IS HAPPENING: Google slashed pricing on its budget AI subscription tier this week, undercutting OpenAI and Anthropic by enough margin to force a response. The move comes as usage-based billing models — like GitHub Copilot's new credit system — reveal that flat-rate AI subscriptions have been loss leaders. Google is betting it can sustain lower prices longer than competitors who don't own the compute stack.

WHY NOW: GitHub's June 1st switch to credit-based billing exposed real costs. Developers are burning through $10 plans in days, not months. Google sees an opening to grab share before competitors adjust pricing or usage caps.

WHY IT MATTERS: AI subscription economics have been subsidised theater. The companies that own chips and data centers can price below cost indefinitely. The ones that rent compute cannot. This is the beginning of a shakeout that will separate vertically integrated players from everyone else.

WHAT THIS MEANS: Expect OpenAI and Anthropic to either raise prices, tighten usage limits, or both within 90 days. The era of "unlimited" AI for $20/month is ending. What replaces it will look more like cloud computing — tiered, metered, and optimised for margin.

WHAT MOST PEOPLE MISS: This isn't just about consumer subscriptions. Enterprise AI contracts are still being negotiated on flat-rate assumptions that no longer hold. Companies signing multi-year deals right now are locking in pricing models that will be obsolete by Q4. The smart move is shorter contracts with usage-based terms, not volume discounts on phantom capacity.

WHAT YOU SHOULD DO:

  • If you are a professional → Audit your AI tool spend now. Track credit burn rates. If you're on a flat-rate plan, calculate what you'd pay under usage-based pricing and budget accordingly.
  • If you are a founder → Renegotiate any AI infrastructure contracts longer than 12 months. Lock in compute commitments with Google or Microsoft if you can — they're the only ones who won't reprice you mid-contract.
  • If you are an investor → Avoid companies with AI-heavy cost structures that don't own their inference stack. The margin compression is coming, and it will be brutal for anyone paying retail rates for compute.

THE CALL: Google just reminded everyone that owning the full stack — chips, data centers, models — is the only defensible position in AI infrastructure. OpenAI's partnership with Microsoft looks weaker every quarter. Anthropic's AWS deal buys them time, but not independence. The next 18 months will separate the platforms from the renters.

ANTHROPIC SHIPS TWO VERSIONS OF THE SAME MODEL — AND ADMITS CAPABILITY CONTROL IS NOW PRODUCT STRATEGY

WHAT IS HAPPENING: Anthropic released Claude Mythos 5 to a restricted set of cybersecurity and government partners, and Claude Fable 5 — a deliberately weakened version — to the public. This is the first time a frontier lab has explicitly forked a model based on risk, not just price or performance. Mythos was previously available only through Anthropic's Cybersecurity Program. Fable is marketed as "safe" and incapable of being used for offensive cyber operations.

WHY NOW: Anthropic has been under pressure from regulators and enterprise customers to demonstrate that it can control model capabilities before deployment. Shipping two versions of the same architecture is a public signal that the company is willing to sacrifice revenue to maintain trust with governments and critical infrastructure customers.

WHY IT MATTERS: This establishes a new norm: frontier models will have restricted and public versions. It also means that the most capable AI systems will be available only to vetted organisations — governments, defense contractors, financial institutions. The gap between what's publicly available and what's privately deployed is about to widen significantly.

WHAT THIS MEANS: If you're building on Claude, you're building on the nerfed version. The real capabilities are behind a vetting process that most startups and SMBs won't pass. This creates a two-tier AI economy: organisations with access to unrestricted models, and everyone else.

WHAT MOST PEOPLE MISS: This isn't just about cybersecurity. It's a template for how all frontier labs will handle capability overhang going forward. OpenAI, Google, and others will follow with similar tiering. The public API will always be 6–12 months behind what governments and select partners have access to. If you're competing against someone with Mythos-level access, you're not competing on level ground.

WHAT YOU SHOULD DO:

  • If you are a professional → Understand which tier your organisation qualifies for. If you're in finance, defense, or critical infrastructure, apply for restricted access now — the vetting process takes months.
  • If you are a founder → Don't build a product that depends on capabilities you don't have guaranteed access to. If your competitive advantage requires Mythos-level reasoning, you need to be in Anthropic's partner program or you're building on sand.
  • If you are an investor → Ask portfolio companies which model tier they're using and whether they have contractual guarantees. A startup building on public APIs is at structural disadvantage to one with restricted access.

THE CALL: Anthropic just made explicit what was already true: the best models are not for sale to the public. This is the right call from a safety perspective, but it creates a permanent moat for incumbents. If you're a startup trying to compete with a company that has Mythos access, you're not just behind on product — you're behind on infrastructure you can't buy.

GM JUST TURNED ELECTRIC CARS INTO GRID INFRASTRUCTURE — AND OPENED A $70B MARKET NOBODY SAW COMING

WHAT IS HAPPENING: GM announced it's developing sodium-ion batteries for stationary energy storage and activating vehicle-to-grid (V2G) charging for GM Energy customers. This isn't a pilot — it's a commercial product. GM is positioning EVs as distributed grid assets that can sell power back during peak demand, and it's building an entirely separate battery chemistry (sodium-ion, not lithium) for data centers and industrial use.

WHY NOW: AI data centers are creating unprecedented electricity demand spikes. Grid operators are paying premium rates for fast-response capacity. GM sees an opportunity to monetise parked EVs as virtual power plants and to sell stationary storage to hyperscalers who can't wait 3–5 years for new grid infrastructure.

WHY IT MATTERS: This reframes what an automaker is. GM is no longer just selling cars — it's selling distributed energy infrastructure. Every EV becomes a grid node. Every battery factory becomes a utility supplier. This is a structural shift in how transportation and energy markets interact.

WHAT THIS MEANS: Utilities, data center operators, and automakers are about to collide in ways that regulatory frameworks aren't prepared for. GM is betting it can move faster than Tesla (which has been talking about V2G for years but hasn't shipped it at scale) and faster than pure-play battery companies (which don't have millions of vehicles already on the road).

WHAT MOST PEOPLE MISS: The sodium-ion play is the sleeper story. Sodium batteries are cheaper, safer, and don't rely on lithium supply chains that China controls. If GM can make sodium-ion work at scale, it breaks the geopolitical stranglehold on battery materials — and it gives the U.S. a domestic energy storage supply chain that doesn't depend on adversarial nations. This is as much a national security move as a commercial one.

WHAT YOU SHOULD DO:

  • If you are a professional → If you work in energy, data centers, or fleet management, model what V2G economics look like for your organisation. The payback period on EVs just shortened significantly if they can generate revenue when parked.
  • If you are a founder → There's a new category emerging: software that optimises when EVs charge and discharge based on grid pricing. If you're in energy tech or fleet software, this is a wedge.
  • If you are an investor → Sodium-ion battery startups just got validated by a major OEM. Look for companies building sodium-ion supply chains or software that manages distributed energy assets. The market just went from speculative to real.

THE CALL: GM is doing what Tesla should have done three years ago. V2G has been vaporware for a decade because no automaker wanted to deal with utility regulations and grid integration complexity. GM just decided the revenue opportunity is worth the pain. If this works, every other OEM will follow within 18 months — and the grid will look fundamentally different by 2030.

JPMORGAN IS DEPLOYING LONG-RUNNING AI AGENTS THIS YEAR — WHICH MEANS THE ENTERPRISE HOLDOUTS JUST BROKE

WHAT IS HAPPENING: JPMorgan Chase announced it will deploy long-running AI agents — systems that operate autonomously across multiple sessions, recover from failures, and make decisions without human intervention — within its operations this year. This is the first major bank to publicly commit to agentic AI in production, not just experimentation.

WHY NOW: The security and governance frameworks that have blocked agentic AI inside large enterprises are finally clearing. JPMorgan has spent 18 months building internal guardrails, audit trails, and kill switches. The fact that they're willing to go public means they believe the risk is manageable — and that competitors are close behind.

WHY IT MATTERS: Banks don't move first on risky technology. If JPMorgan is deploying agents, it means the enterprise AI market just shifted from "proof of concept" to "production deployment." Every other Fortune 500 company will now face board-level questions about why they're not doing the same.

WHAT THIS MEANS: The market for agentic AI infrastructure — orchestration layers, monitoring tools, governance frameworks — is about to explode. Companies that have been selling "AI assistants" will pivot to "AI agents" within 90 days. Expect a wave of acquisitions as enterprise software vendors scramble to add agentic capabilities.

WHAT MOST PEOPLE MISS: JPMorgan's move signals that the liability and compliance questions around autonomous AI have been answered — at least well enough for a bank's legal team to sign off. That's a bigger unlock than any model improvement. The bottleneck was never capability; it was governance. That bottleneck just cleared.

WHAT YOU SHOULD DO:

  • If you are a professional → If you work in ops, finance, or compliance at a large company, start documenting which workflows could be automated by agents. The RFPs are coming, and you want to be ready with use cases.
  • If you are a founder → If you're building AI tooling, add agent orchestration and audit logging to your roadmap immediately. Enterprise buyers will require both within six months.
  • If you are an investor → Look for companies building governance and observability layers for agentic AI. The picks-and-shovels play here is monitoring and compliance, not the agents themselves.

THE CALL: This is the moment enterprise AI goes from "interesting" to "mandatory." JPMorgan just gave every other bank, insurer, and asset manager permission to deploy agents. The companies that move in the next 12 months will have a 2–3 year operational advantage over the ones that wait. This is the enterprise AI inflection point.
