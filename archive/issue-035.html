TOP SIGNALS THAT MATTER

THE SPACEX IPO IS A TROJAN HORSE FOR DEFENSE DEPENDENCY

WHAT IS HAPPENING: SpaceX disclosed in its IPO filing that government contracts generated one-fifth of its 2025 revenue. This week alone, the company secured $10.6 billion in new federal deals: $6.45 billion from Space Force and $4.16 billion to build missile-tracking satellites for Trump's "Golden Dome" defense system. The timing — weeks before the public offering — signals SpaceX is positioning itself as critical national infrastructure, not just a commercial launch provider.

WHY NOW: The IPO filing deadline forced disclosure of revenue composition that SpaceX has historically kept opaque. The Golden Dome contract award, announced Friday, came after months of Pentagon deliberation and represents the largest single satellite contract in Space Force history.

WHY IT MATTERS: SpaceX is no longer a private space company that happens to work with government. It's a defense contractor that happens to launch commercial payloads. That changes valuation models, regulatory exposure, and strategic optionality. When 20% of revenue comes from contracts that require security clearances and can't be easily replaced, you're not disrupting aerospace — you're embedded in it.

WHAT THIS MEANS: The IPO will price SpaceX as a dual-use infrastructure play, not a pure innovation story. Expect defense-focused institutional buyers to dominate the allocation, and expect Musk's geopolitical positioning to matter more to the stock price than Starship test flights.

WHAT MOST PEOPLE MISS: The Golden Dome contract isn't just revenue — it's a moat. Building a constellation of missile-tracking satellites creates vendor lock-in at the national security level. No competitor can bid on follow-on work without first replicating SpaceX's launch cadence, satellite manufacturing scale, and existing orbital infrastructure. This contract effectively closes the door on new entrants to military space for a decade.

WHAT YOU SHOULD DO:

  • If you are a professional → If you're in aerospace or defense tech, understand that SpaceX's IPO will reset compensation benchmarks and talent expectations across the sector. Companies that can't offer equity upside tied to government contracts will struggle to retain senior engineers.
  • If you are a founder → If you're building dual-use technology (AI, robotics, materials), study how SpaceX structured its government relationships early. Revenue concentration risk becomes strategic advantage when the customer can't switch vendors.
  • If you are an investor → Price SpaceX as a defense prime with commercial upside, not the reverse. Compare multiples to Lockheed and Northrop, not Tesla. The 20% government revenue figure is a floor, not a ceiling — and it comes with margin profiles commercial launch can't match.

THE CALL: Musk spent a decade positioning SpaceX as the scrappy disruptor taking on Boeing and Lockheed. The IPO filing reveals he won by becoming them. The Golden Dome contract is the tell: SpaceX isn't disrupting the military-industrial complex, it's the next generation of it. Investors who price this as a tech IPO will miss the point entirely.

AI AGENTS ARE FAILING IN PRODUCTION — AND IT'S NOT THE MODELS

WHAT IS HAPPENING: Enterprise AI deployments are stalling at scale, but not because of model performance. Companies are hitting a permissions bottleneck: agentic workflows can't answer "what is this agent allowed to access, on whose behalf, and how does the system verify that?" Systems crash, lose state across sessions, and fail when they encounter access control boundaries. Workday's response is to rebuild permissioning infrastructure specifically for agents. Separately, researchers are now automating the design of reasoning strategies, cutting token usage by 69.5% while maintaining performance — addressing the cost side of long-running workflows.

WHY NOW: The first wave of enterprise agents has been in production long enough to fail predictably. IT and security teams are rejecting deployments not because agents don't work, but because they can't be safely contained. At the same time, inference costs for multi-step reasoning are forcing companies to choose between capability and budget.

WHY IT MATTERS: This is the difference between demos and production systems. An agent that can draft an email is a toy. An agent that can approve a purchase order, update a CRM, and trigger a contract workflow is a liability unless you can audit every action it takes and revoke access in real time. Permissioning isn't an IT problem — it's the product problem that determines whether agents ship at all.

WHAT THIS MEANS: The next 18 months of enterprise AI will be defined by infrastructure, not models. Companies that solve agent permissioning, state persistence, and cost control will capture deployment budgets. Those that keep optimising for benchmark performance will keep building demos no one can use.

WHAT MOST PEOPLE MISS: The token usage breakthrough isn't about making models cheaper — it's about making long-running agents economically viable. A 69.5% reduction in tokens means workflows that were previously too expensive to run in production (multi-step research, iterative code generation, complex data analysis) suddenly fit inside operational budgets. That unlocks an entirely new class of use cases that weren't possible six months ago, regardless of model capability.

WHAT YOU SHOULD DO:

  • If you are a professional → If you're deploying AI agents, stop treating permissioning as an afterthought. Build access control into the agent architecture from day one, or plan to rebuild the entire system when security flags it in production.
  • If you are a founder → If you're building agent infrastructure, the wedge is permissioning and state management, not model wrappers. Enterprises will pay for systems that let them safely delegate authority to agents without creating audit nightmares.
  • If you are an investor → Look for companies solving agent reliability and cost control, not model performance. The bottleneck has shifted from "can the model do this?" to "can we afford to run it, and can we trust it not to break?" Infrastructure plays that answer those questions will capture more value than the next model lab.

THE CALL: The AI industry spent two years obsessing over model benchmarks and six months discovering that production deployment is a completely different game. Permissioning is boring. State persistence is boring. Token cost optimisation is boring. But boring infrastructure is what separates products that ship from products that get stuck in pilot purgatory. The companies that solve these problems won't get the headlines — but they'll get the revenue.

PINTEREST PROVED YOU DON'T NEED FRONTIER MODELS — YOU NEED FRONTIER ECONOMICS

WHAT IS HAPPENING: Pinterest cut AI inference costs by 90% and improved performance by rebuilding Qwen3-VL's vision layer with proprietary embeddings instead of using the model's native vision encoder. At 620 million monthly users, calling a frontier model for every image recommendation was financially unsustainable. CTO Matt Madrigal's team stripped out the expensive components, kept the reasoning layer, and replaced the rest with custom-trained embeddings optimised for Pinterest's specific use case.

WHY NOW: Inference costs at scale finally exceeded the cost of custom engineering. For most companies, frontier models are still cheaper than building alternatives. For Pinterest, serving 620 million users meant every basis point of cost reduction translated to millions in annual savings — enough to justify the engineering investment.

WHY IT MATTERS: This is the playbook for every company operating AI at consumer scale. Frontier models are extraordinary, but they're built for generality, not efficiency. If you have proprietary data, high query volume, and a narrow use case, you can beat frontier models on cost and performance by rebuilding the parts that matter and discarding the rest.

WHAT THIS MEANS: The next wave of AI cost optimisation won't come from model labs — it will come from companies with scale and domain expertise gutting general-purpose models and replacing components with task-specific alternatives. Frontier models become starting points, not endpoints.

WHAT MOST PEOPLE MISS: Pinterest didn't just cut costs — they improved performance. That's the key insight. Frontier models are over-engineered for most production use cases. By removing capabilities Pinterest didn't need (general vision understanding) and optimising for what they did need (visual similarity for recommendations), they built a system that's both cheaper and better. This isn't a compromise. It's a better product.

WHAT YOU SHOULD DO:

  • If you are a professional → If you're running AI in production at scale, audit your inference costs and identify where you're paying for generality you don't need. The ROI on custom model optimisation is now measurable in quarters, not years.
  • If you are a founder → If you're building AI products, plan for a two-stage architecture: start with frontier models to prove the use case, then rebuild the expensive parts with custom components once you have volume and data. Don't over-optimise early, but don't stay on frontier models forever.
  • If you are an investor → Companies with proprietary data and high query volume have a structural cost advantage in AI that will compound over time. Pinterest's playbook is replicable for any company with scale and domain expertise. Look for businesses that can afford to invest in custom optimisation — they'll have better unit economics than competitors stuck on API calls.

THE CALL: The AI industry has spent two years in a race to build the most capable models. Pinterest just proved the more important race is building the most economically sustainable ones. Frontier models are incredible, but they're also expensive and over-engineered for most real-world applications. The companies that win won't be the ones using the best models — they'll be the ones using the right models, rebuilt for their specific economics. That's a very different game.

MARKET MOVES THAT MATTER

The AI infrastructure trade unwound violently this week. Nvidia dropped 16% in a single session — its worst day since the pandemic — as investors repriced the assumption that frontier model training would require infinite compute scaling. The trigger was China's DeepSeek, but the underlying anxiety was already present: if inference costs are falling 90% (see: Pinterest) and reasoning strategies are cutting token usage by 69.5%, the path to AI profitability doesn't run through buying more GPUs. Broadcom and other AI infrastructure plays followed Nvidia down. This isn't a sector rotation — it's a recalibration of how much compute the AI economy actually needs.

Meanwhile, software had its best month since 2001. Snowflake and Okta posted record single-day gains as investors rotated into companies that can deploy AI without rebuilding their infrastructure stack. The "SaaSpocalypse" narrative — that AI would collapse software margins — has reversed. Turns out AI is a feature that increases willingness to pay, not a substitute that commoditises the product. Dell surged 32% on AI server revenue growth of 757%, proving that even in a world of falling inference costs, someone still has to sell the hardware.

Commodity markets reflected geopolitical instability and tariff uncertainty. Arabica coffee hit record highs on U.S.-Colombia tariff tensions. Gold and silver both sold off mid-week as the dollar strengthened against the Swiss franc following a surprise 50bp rate cut from the Swiss National Bank. Oil remained range-bound despite ongoing U.S.-Iran negotiations, which ended this week without a deal. Trump's statement on Truth Social left terms unclear, and markets priced in continued uncertainty.

NEUTRAL:

AVOID:

BOTTOM LINE

The AI economy just split into two games: one where companies compete on model capability, and one where they compete on deployment economics. The latter is where the money is.
