TOP SIGNALS THAT MATTER

THE AI HARDWARE GOLD RUSH IS CREATING STRANGE NEW WINNERS — AND BRUTAL LOSERS

WHAT IS HAPPENING: IBM stock suffered its worst single-day loss on record — down 25% — after warning that enterprise clients are slashing software and infrastructure spending to redirect capital toward AI hardware purchases. Meanwhile, Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan posted record quarterly revenues, driven by a surge in M&A advisory and trading volumes tied to AI infrastructure deals. SK Hynix shares jumped 11% in a single session as Asian semiconductor stocks rallied hard.

WHY NOW: We're hitting the point where AI capex is no longer additive — it's substitutional. Enterprise IT budgets haven't expanded; they've been reallocated. The companies selling picks and shovels are winning. The companies selling legacy infrastructure are getting gutted.

WHY IT MATTERS: This is the first clear signal that AI spending is cannibalising established tech revenue streams at scale. It's not a rising tide lifting all boats — it's a zero-sum reallocation of finite enterprise budgets. The market is starting to price in who wins and who gets left behind.

WHAT THIS MEANS: If you're a tech company that doesn't directly enable AI compute, training, or inference, you're now in a defensive posture. Software margins are compressing. Infrastructure spend is being redirected. The safe assumption is that your growth rate is someone else's AI budget.

WHAT MOST PEOPLE MISS: The real story isn't IBM's bad quarter — it's that Wall Street banks are becoming the primary beneficiaries of AI capex. Every data centre deal, every chip fab expansion, every AI startup fundraise generates fees for Goldman and JPMorgan. They're not building AI — they're taxing the gold rush. That's a better business.

WHAT YOU SHOULD DO:

  • If you are a professional → If your company sells enterprise software or cloud infrastructure that isn't AI-native, start building optionality now. The budget reallocation is structural, not cyclical.
  • If you are a founder → Assume your customers' IT budgets are flat or shrinking. If your product doesn't directly reduce AI costs or enable AI workflows, you're swimming upstream.
  • If you are an investor → Overweight financial services exposure to AI capex flows. Underweight legacy enterprise software that can't articulate a clear AI wedge.

THE CALL: IBM's collapse is a warning shot. The AI boom isn't lifting the entire tech sector — it's redistributing value violently. The companies that thought they could ride adjacency are about to learn they're in the blast radius instead.

CHINA'S GROWTH MODEL IS BREAKING — AND THE WORLD ISN'T READY

WHAT IS HAPPENING: China posted 4.3% GDP growth in Q2 2026, the slowest quarterly expansion since 2022 and a clear miss against Beijing's 4.5–5% full-year target. The slowdown is driven by collapsing domestic investment and weak consumer demand, even as exports remain strong. The Iran conflict and rising oil prices are compounding pressure on an economy already struggling with structural headwinds.

WHY NOW: This isn't a temporary dip — it's the first hard evidence that China's post-COVID recovery has stalled out. Investment is slumping despite aggressive stimulus signals, which means confidence among Chinese businesses and households is broken. Beijing set its least ambitious growth target in decades, and it's still missing.

WHY IT MATTERS: China has been the primary engine of global growth for two decades. If it can't hit 5% anymore — and can't stimulate domestic demand without triggering capital flight or debt crises — the rest of the world loses its growth backstop. Commodity exporters, luxury goods makers, and anyone counting on Chinese consumer spending is about to reprice risk.

WHAT THIS MEANS: The era of assuming China will always hit its growth target is over. The government's toolkit — infrastructure spending, credit expansion, currency management — is producing diminishing returns. The next phase is either a major structural reform (unlikely under Xi) or a prolonged Japan-style stagnation.

WHAT MOST PEOPLE MISS: The real danger isn't that China is slowing — it's that China is slowing while the US is in a shooting war with Iran and Europe is still rebuilding its defence posture. There's no global growth engine left. The synchronised slowdown scenario that markets have been discounting is now the base case.

WHAT YOU SHOULD DO:

  • If you are a professional → If your company has meaningful China exposure — either as a market or a supply chain dependency — model downside scenarios now. The optimistic case is off the table.
  • If you are a founder → Don't build your growth model around Chinese consumer demand. The structural tailwinds that existed five years ago have reversed.
  • If you are an investor → Reduce exposure to companies dependent on Chinese domestic consumption. Overweight companies that benefit from supply chain diversification out of China.

THE CALL: China's slowdown is the most underpriced macro risk in global markets right now. Everyone is focused on the Fed, the Iran war, and AI capex. Almost no one is pricing in a scenario where the world's second-largest economy can't grow faster than 4% — and can't fix it without breaking something else.

THE US-IRAN WAR JUST RESTARTED — AND IT'S EXPOSING HOW LITTLE LEVERAGE AMERICA ACTUALLY HAS

WHAT IS HAPPENING: The US and Iran resumed open hostilities over the Strait of Hormuz after a four-month ceasefire collapsed. The US reinstated a naval blockade of Iranian ports, Iran attacked two tankers and fired on US military sites, and Trump threatened to bomb bridges and power plants unless Tehran returns to negotiations. Then, within 24 hours, Trump backed down on his demand that Iran pay tolls for Hormuz passage — a retreat that signals he can't enforce the terms he's threatening over.

WHY NOW: The ceasefire was always fragile, but it broke because neither side got what it wanted. Iran didn't get sanctions relief. The US didn't get a long-term deal. Now both sides are back to coercive tactics, except the US is discovering that blockades and airstrikes aren't producing the negotiating leverage Trump expected.

WHY IT MATTERS: The Strait of Hormuz is the world's most critical oil chokepoint — roughly 20% of global oil supply transits through it. A sustained conflict here doesn't just spike oil prices (which it already has) — it fractures global energy markets, forces Europe and Asia to choose sides, and exposes the limits of US military power when the adversary is willing to absorb punishment.

WHAT THIS MEANS: Trump's retreat on tolls is the tell. He threatened infrastructure strikes, reinstated the blockade, and then immediately walked back a core demand because he realised he couldn't enforce it without triggering a full-scale war he doesn't want. That's not strength — that's bluffing with a weak hand. Iran now knows it.

WHAT MOST PEOPLE MISS: The real story isn't the military exchange — it's that the US is struggling to end a war it's been fighting for four months. Trump campaigned on ending foreign entanglements. Instead, he's stuck in an escalatory cycle with no clear exit and no international coalition backing him. Europe is on the sidelines. China is watching. The US is isolated.

WHAT YOU SHOULD DO:

  • If you are a professional → If your business has energy cost exposure, lock in hedges now. Oil volatility is going to persist as long as this conflict drags on.
  • If you are a founder → If you're in logistics, manufacturing, or anything with complex international supply chains, model scenarios where Hormuz is intermittently closed. It's no longer a tail risk.
  • If you are an investor → Overweight energy and defence. Underweight anything with tight margins that can't pass through oil price shocks.

THE CALL: Trump's Hormuz retreat is the most important geopolitical signal of the year. It reveals that the US doesn't have the leverage it thought it had — and that adversaries are learning to call bluffs. The era of American coercive diplomacy working by default is over.

MARKET MOVES THAT MATTER

Oil prices jumped sharply this week as the US-Iran conflict reignited, with Brent crude pushing back toward levels not seen since the initial flare-up in March. The move isn't just about supply risk — it's about the realisation that this conflict has no clear off-ramp. Heating oil customers in the UK are now receiving compensation after price spikes earlier this year, a sign that energy volatility is starting to trigger regulatory and political responses in consumer markets.

Asian equities rallied hard mid-week, led by a 11% single-day surge in SK Hynix and broad strength across semiconductor names. The move tracked a rebound in US chip stocks after a brutal selloff earlier in the week, but the whipsaw volatility signals that investors are still trying to figure out whether AI capex is peaking or just pausing. TSMC reported a 68% surge in June revenue ahead of its Q2 earnings, which helped stabilise sentiment — but the real test is whether that revenue growth translates into margin expansion or just reflects a temporary restocking cycle.

Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan both hit record highs after blowout earnings, with Goldman posting its strongest quarter in years on the back of surging M&A and trading volumes. The message is clear: Wall Street is printing money from the AI infrastructure boom, even as the companies actually building AI infrastructure (like IBM) are getting hammered. It's a reminder that in every gold rush, the people selling shovels and financing deals often do better than the miners.

OPPORTUNITIES

  • AI cost management tooling — 1Password just launched an AI spend management product, betting that token consumption is about to become a major enterprise budget line item. They're right. Every company using multiple AI vendors is flying blind on costs right now.
  • Model routing infrastructure — ACRouter and similar tools are showing that dynamically routing prompts to the right AI model can cut costs by 2.6x. This is early-stage infrastructure that will become table stakes within 18 months.
  • Data infrastructure for AI agents — Oxylabs just raised $130M at a $3.6B valuation because AI agents need real-time data at scale. The companies that can deliver clean, structured, real-time data feeds are going to be critical infrastructure.
  • Humanoid robotics in China — Chinese humanoid startups are rushing to IPO as investors eye exits. LimX Dynamics is the latest to raise capital. The sector is frothy, but the underlying thesis — that China will lead in robotics manufacturing — is sound.
  • Supply chain diversification plays — As China's growth slows and geopolitical risk rises, companies that enable supply chain shifts out of China (logistics, manufacturing, trade finance) are structurally positioned to win.

MARKET POSITIONING

OVERWEIGHT:

  • Financial services with AI capex exposure (Goldman, JPMorgan)
  • Semiconductor capital equipment and memory (SK Hynix, TSMC)
  • Energy and defence (structural tailwinds from Iran conflict)
  • AI infrastructure tooling (cost management, model routing, data feeds)
  • Supply chain diversification enablers

NEUTRAL:

  • Chinese consumer discretionary (wait for clearer stimulus signals)
  • US tech mega-caps (AI capex is still strong, but substitution risk is rising)
  • European equities (geopolitically sidelined, but not broken)

AVOID:

  • Legacy enterprise software without AI integration (IBM is the canary)
  • China domestic demand plays (structural slowdown is real)
  • Companies with high energy cost exposure and no pricing power
  • Anything dependent on Strait of Hormuz remaining open
  • Consumer discretionary in markets with rising oil prices

BOTTOM LINE

The AI boom is no longer lifting all boats — it's reallocating capital violently, and the companies that thought adjacency was enough are getting crushed. Meanwhile, the macro backdrop is darkening: China can't hit its growth targets, the US is stuck in an unwinnable war with Iran, and the global growth engine just stalled out. Position accordingly.
